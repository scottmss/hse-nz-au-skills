# The Energy Wheel — the ten categories

The jurisdiction-neutral core of `energy-based-safety-specialist`. Ten categories of hazardous
energy; work through **all ten** for any task, and record the ones you ruled out. The regulatory
anchor for New Zealand is in `nz-gpg.md`; the classification thresholds are in
`high-energy-thresholds.md`; the control test is in `direct-controls.md`.

> **Verify-pointer, not a reproduction.** This is a summary of the CHASNZ guide *High Energy
> Hazards on Construction Sites in New Zealand* (v2.0, February 2024) and the CSRA/EEI research
> behind it, extended to NZ/AU work generally. Read the source for the full hazard and control
> tables. **Not legal advice.**
>
> **Sources last verified: 2026-09-03.**

## Why a wheel and not a list

Unaided hazard recognition is poor and *systematically* poor. NZ construction research puts
identification at **under half** the hazards actually present — roughly a third missed because of
how visual attention and expectation work, and a further fifth missed because the work changed
from what was planned. The failure is rarely one overlooked item; it is a **whole category** that
nobody was looking for. A fixed wheel of ten forces the sweep to be complete rather than
associative, which is exactly what a free-form "what could go wrong?" cannot do.

Energy is also the right organising unit because it is **measurable**. "Struck by" is an outcome;
"a 300 kg beam suspended 2 m above the crew" is an energy you can compute, compare and control.

## The ten categories

Order matters only for consistency — use the same order every time so a missed category is
visible.

### 1. Gravity — *the attraction of mass toward the earth*
Potential energy released as something (or someone) descends.

- **Typical high-energy sources.** A person working above ~1.2 m — platforms, roofs, mezzanines,
  truck decks, scaffolds, ladders, open penetrations, voids, steep slopes. Suspended loads and
  lifting operations. Materials, tools and debris that can fall onto people below. Unstable ground,
  excavation faces and stockpiles that can collapse or engulf.
- **Direct controls look like.** The work relocated to ground level; a physical guardrail or a
  covered and secured penetration; a working platform with edge protection; a designed exclusion
  zone under a suspended load, physically demarcated; shoring, benching or battering that holds the
  face regardless of who walks near it.
- **Commonly miscounted as direct.** A harness and lanyard (depends on correct clipping every
  time — see `direct-controls.md` on why fall arrest is usually *alternate*), tool lanyards,
  "keep out of the drop zone" briefings, hard hats.

### 2. Mechanical — *rotation, vibration, tension or compression*
Energy stored or transmitted in machinery and in loaded components.

- **Typical high-energy sources.** Mobile and fixed plant — excavators, loaders, dozers, cranes,
  crushers, batching and asphalt plant. Rotating and reciprocating machinery. Powered hand tools
  with exposed rotating parts. Stored energy in springs, hydraulics and accumulators.
- **Direct controls look like.** Fixed or interlocked guarding over the moving part; energy
  isolation and lockout/tagout with stored energy dissipated and verified at zero; plant operated
  within its engineered limits with stability systems deployed.
- **Commonly miscounted as direct.** Operator certification, pre-start checklists, proximity
  alarms, "trained and competent" operators, maintenance schedules.

### 3. Motion — *change in position of objects or substances*
Kinetic energy of things already moving. Distinct from gravity (falling) and mechanical
(rotating in place).

- **Typical high-energy sources.** Vehicles and mobile plant moving anywhere near people on foot;
  site and public traffic; reversing; machinery in transit; projectiles from cutting, grinding,
  nail guns, chipping and compressed air; unexpected movement of a lifted or propped load.
- **Direct controls look like.** Hard physical separation of people and plant — barriers, dedicated
  and enclosed pedestrian routes, a positively controlled work zone that a vehicle cannot enter;
  engineered traffic separation rather than a plan on paper; interlocks that prevent movement while
  a person is in the zone.
- **Commonly miscounted as direct.** Spotters, hi-vis, reversing beepers, cameras and proximity
  sensors, traffic management plans, speed limits, "make eye contact with the operator".

### 4. Sound — *audible vibration from objects in contact*
The one category most often skipped, because its harm is usually chronic.

- **Typical high-energy sources.** Concrete demolition and breaking, jackhammers, pile driving,
  grinding and cutting, heavy plant, fixed plant. Also **hand-arm and whole-body vibration** from
  power tools and equipment.
- **Threshold note.** Sound and vibration are health hazards — they are high-energy when exposure
  **exceeds the workplace exposure standard**, not by joule count. Acute rupture-level noise
  (~150 dB) is rare but real.
- **Direct controls look like.** Eliminating or substituting the noisy or vibrating process;
  isolating the source or the people from it; engineered attenuation and damping at source; tools
  selected for low vibration emission.
- **Commonly miscounted as direct.** Hearing protection, dosimetry, audiometry and health
  monitoring, job rotation, "wear your earmuffs".

### 5. Pressure — *liquid or gas compressed, or under vacuum*
Stored energy proportional to both pressure and contained volume.

- **Typical high-energy sources.** Hydraulic systems, compressors and air receivers, pneumatic
  tools and hoses, gas cylinders, boilers and hot-water systems, pressurised process piping,
  buried pressurised services. Also **stored tension** in cables, ropes, winches, and
  post-tensioned concrete.
- **Direct controls look like.** Depressurising and positively isolating before the work; verified
  pressure relief; physical isolation of people from the release path; barriers preventing approach
  to tensioned components; design and installation to the governing standard.
- **Commonly miscounted as direct.** Safety labelling, signage, training on the equipment,
  inspection regimes, "don't stand in line with the hose".

### 6. Radiation — *elements emitting ions or atomic particles*
Includes non-ionising and artificial optical sources, which are far more common on ordinary sites.

- **Typical high-energy sources.** Nuclear density gauges, industrial radiography and x-ray
  inspection (ionising). Arc welding and oxy-acetylene flash, lasers used in survey and layout,
  UV/IR sources, HID lighting (non-ionising/optical). Solar UV outdoors.
- **Direct controls look like.** Shielding that absorbs or blocks the emission; substituting a
  non-radiating method; physical barriers holding a safe distance; interlocks and power isolation
  for maintenance; guarding and collimation of optical sources.
- **Commonly miscounted as direct.** Dosimeters, permits, calibration records, welding helmets and
  safety glasses, sunscreen, exposure monitoring.

### 7. Temperature — *thermal energy differing from the human body*
Both directions — hot and cold.

- **Typical high-energy sources.** Welding, torch cutting and grinding; hot bitumen and its plant;
  steam and hot water; fire with a sustained fuel source; work in direct summer sun on roofs and
  paving. Cold — refrigerated buildings and cold stores, containers, winter framing/roofing work.
- **Direct controls look like.** Isolating the thermal source from the work and public areas;
  remote or automated processes that remove people from the exposure; engineered shading, heating or
  cooling of the work area; heated refuge for cold work.
- **Commonly miscounted as direct.** Thermal PPE and gloves, hydration and break schedules,
  sunscreen mandates, heat-stress awareness training, rescheduling to cooler hours.

### 8. Chemical — *reactive elements in the environment*
Assessed against exposure standards, not joules.

- **Typical high-energy sources.** Toxic, corrosive, carcinogenic and teratogenic substances;
  fibres, dusts, vapours and fumes — **respirable crystalline silica** and **asbestos** foremost in
  NZ/AU construction; welding fume; sensitisers causing occupational asthma and dermatitis;
  flammables and reactive combinations; oxygen displacement.
- **Threshold note.** High-energy when exposure exceeds the **WES/exposure standard**, when oxygen
  falls below ~16%, or for strongly corrosive material (pH below ~2 or above ~12.5). Involve an
  occupational hygienist for anything marginal.
- **Direct controls look like.** Eliminating or substituting the substance or the process that
  generates it (on-tool water suppression, off-site cutting, a non-silica product); fully enclosed
  handling; local exhaust ventilation designed and verified for the source.
- **Commonly miscounted as direct.** SDS availability, RPE and fit-testing, decontamination
  procedures, exposure monitoring, health surveillance, certified-handler training.

### 9. Biological — *living organisms posing a health risk*
- **Typical high-energy sources.** Contaminated soils and surfaces; sewage and stagnant water;
  human pathogens; zoonoses (**leptospirosis** in NZ, **Q fever** in AU); mould spores, prions,
  pollens and indoor air quality; sharps.
- **Direct controls look like.** Removal or remediation of the contaminated material; full
  containment and enclosure; engineered ventilation and filtration; a physical barrier between the
  organism and the person.
- **Commonly miscounted as direct.** Vaccination, hygiene facilities, PPE, health monitoring,
  "wash your hands" briefings.

### 10. Electrical — *the presence of an electrical charge or current*
- **Typical high-energy sources.** Live wiring, switchboards and site services; overhead and
  underground services struck during excavation or plant movement; faulty or damaged tools and
  leads; arc flash and blast; static discharge.
- **Direct controls look like.** De-energising, isolating, locking out and **proving dead**;
  physical insulated shields or barriers preventing contact with live parts; verified service
  location and positive isolation before ground penetration; engineered clearance from overhead
  lines. **RCDs are the standard edge case** — treat as direct only where the installation is
  verified and tested, and never as a substitute for isolation.
- **Commonly miscounted as direct.** Permits, arc-flash PPE, test-and-tag stickers, cable maps
  and "before you dig" plans without physical proving, competent-electrician assurances.

## What is *not* on the wheel

**Workload, time pressure, stress, fatigue, impairment and the social conditions of the work are
not energy sources and must not be scored as hazards on the wheel.** They belong in the assessment
for a different reason: they are the strongest modifiers of the human error that the
direct-control test explicitly assumes will happen. Their presence is an argument for **demanding
harder direct controls**, not for adding rows to the energy sweep. Route them to
`../../psychosocial-risk-specialist/`.

## Using the wheel with a bow tie

The two methods answer different questions and compose well:

| | Energy wheel | Bow tie |
|---|---|---|
| **Starts from** | The energy present in the work as observed | A named hazard and its loss-of-control event |
| **Filters by** | Energy magnitude (~1,500 J) | Credibility of threat and consequence lines |
| **Control test** | Targeted, effective, error-tolerant | Hierarchy of controls, criticality, assurance |
| **Best at** | Finding what was missed; killing weak controls | Telling the whole risk story; escalation factors |

Practical sequence: **sweep the wheel first** to find every high-energy source, then build the bow
tie for the ones that matter, using the direct-control test to nominate critical controls. See
`../../critical-risk-manager/`.
