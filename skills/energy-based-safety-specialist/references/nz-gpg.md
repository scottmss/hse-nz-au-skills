# Energy-based safety — New Zealand anchor (CHASNZ) and the AU position

The region-specific layer for `energy-based-safety-specialist`. The method itself is
jurisdiction-neutral and lives in `energy-wheel.md`, `high-energy-thresholds.md` and
`direct-controls.md`; this file records **what the guidance actually is, who publishes it, and
what legal weight it does and does not carry**.

> **Not legal advice.** Guidance changes. Every document below is a **verify-pointer** — confirm
> the current edition before relying on it. Deep statutory duty detail lives in
> `../../worksafe-nz-specialist/` (NZ) and `../../safework-au-specialist/` (AU).

## The NZ frame — good practice, not law

- Energy-based safety has **no statutory basis** in New Zealand. No provision of **HSWA 2015** or
  its regulations mentions the 1,500 J threshold, direct controls, the energy wheel or HECA.
- The duty is unchanged: a PCBU must ensure health and safety **so far as is reasonably practicable
  (SFAIRP)** — eliminate risk SFAIRP, otherwise minimise it SFAIRP.
- What the energy wheel *does* is help discharge that duty. It is the NZ construction sector's
  published articulation of good practice for finding fatal-risk hazards and testing controls.
- **Why that matters legally.** When assessing what is reasonably practicable, the regulator and
  the courts look to legislation, approved codes of practice, and **industry guidance from a
  recognised source**. CHASNZ states in the guide's foreword that **WorkSafe NZ sees CHASNZ as the
  provider of industry guidance for the construction industry**. Guidance from a recognised
  industry body is therefore evidence of what good practice looks like — persuasive, not binding,
  and not a substitute for the ACOPs and regulations that do bind.

Put plainly: **using the energy wheel does not discharge a duty, and not using it does not breach
one** — but a control set that fails the direct-control test is hard to defend as reasonably
practicable when a workable engineered control existed.

## Who CHASNZ is

**Construction Health and Safety New Zealand (CHASNZ)** is an industry-led **registered charitable
trust** supporting the building and construction sector. It publishes sector programmes
(Tōtika, ConstructSafe, Work Should Not Hurt, Lead On) alongside the energy-based safety resources.
It is **not a regulator** and its material is **not an Approved Code of Practice**.

## The source documents

The primary NZ document:

- **High Energy Hazards on Construction Sites in New Zealand — A guide to assist in the
  identification of 'Stuff That Can Kill You' (S.T.C.K.Y)**, CHASNZ, **Version 2.0, February 2024**.
  Ten energy categories, each with typical high-energy hazards, where they appear in construction,
  S.T.C.K.Y examples, direct controls and alternate controls.

The research base (attributed by CHASNZ to **Dr Matt Hallowell**, **Construction Safety Research
Alliance**, University of Colorado):

- **Erkal, E. D. O., & Hallowell, M. (2023).** *Moving Beyond TRIR — Measuring & Monitoring Safety
  Performance With High-Energy Control Assessments.* Professional Safety, 68(05), 26–35. CHASNZ
  hosts a copy at https://cms.chasnz.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/PSJ-Energy-Wheel.pdf
- **EEI Safety Classification and Learning (SCL) Model** (2020) and **High-Energy Control
  Assessments (HECA)** — the North American utility-sector implementation, source of the
  high-energy icons and the HECA rulebook. https://www.eei.org

**Caveat on the research base.** The underlying injury study did not cover every energy source, and
the guide extends the thresholds to all physical hazards by assumption. CHASNZ notes that research
on **alternate controls** was still in progress when v2.0 was published. Treat the framework as
strong and well-evidenced on the gravity/motion side and more assumption-driven at the edges.

## Why NZ construction adopted it

The recognition problem is the stated reason. Figures reported in NZ industry material put hazard
identification on building sites at **only around 45%** of the hazards actually present — roughly
**35%** missed because of how human visual attention and expectation work, and about **20%** missed
because work is unpredictable and changes from what was planned. A fixed ten-category sweep is the
countermeasure to a failure of *attention*, which more exhortation cannot fix.

## How it sits with the rest of the NZ stack

| Instrument | Weight | Role here |
|---|---|---|
| **HSWA 2015** and regulations | Binding law | The duty. Unchanged by this method. |
| **Approved Codes of Practice** (e.g. Cranes, Load-lifting Rigging) | Admissible in proceedings; compliance is evidence of compliance with the duty | The control baseline for their specific activity |
| **WorkSafe good practice guidelines** | Regulator's view of good practice | What "reasonably practicable" normally looks like |
| **CHASNZ energy-based safety** | Recognised industry guidance | Finding hazards and pressure-testing the controls above |

Sequence for NZ construction work: **the ACOP or GPG sets what is required; the energy wheel checks
whether what you actually have would survive an ordinary human mistake.** The wheel is never the
answer to "what does the law require here?" — route that to `../../worksafe-nz-specialist/` and, for
the construction-project regime, `../../construction-specialist/`.

## The Australian position

- **There is no Australian equivalent code.** Energy-based safety is not referenced in the model WHS
  Act, the WHS Regulations, or any model Code of Practice, and Safe Work Australia does not publish
  a version of it.
- The compliance baseline in AU remains the **model Codes of Practice** (and, in Victoria, the
  separate **OHS Act 2004** and its compliance codes) — which are **admissible in proceedings** and
  carry weight the CHASNZ material does not.
- Energy-based safety is nonetheless in wide voluntary use in Australian construction, resources and
  utilities, typically inside corporate **SIF-prevention** programmes and often via the CSRA/EEI
  lineage rather than CHASNZ.
- **Practical rule for AU work:** use the model Code to establish *what is required*, then use the
  energy wheel and the direct-control test to check *whether what is in place would actually hold*.
  Never cite CHASNZ as authority for an Australian control expectation. Jurisdiction and regulator
  detail is in `../../safework-au-specialist/`.

## Terminology drift to watch

- **S.T.C.K.Y** ("stuff that can kill you") in the CHASNZ guide appears as **STKY** ("stuff that
  kills you") in the EEI material. Same concept.
- **"Alternate controls"** (CHASNZ) vs **"indirect"** or **"other controls"** in some corporate
  adaptations. Same concept — but check the local definition has not been quietly widened.
- **"Direct control"** is the term most at risk of local dilution. If an organisation's register
  counts harnesses, spotters or permits as direct controls, it has adopted the vocabulary without
  the method, and its HECA-equivalent score means nothing. See `heca-scl.md` on definition drift.
