# Residential construction — roles, responsibilities and the ACOP (NZ)

Flagship reference for `construction-specialist` on **residential construction**. Grounded in
WorkSafe NZ's approved code of practice (ACOP) **"Identifying health and safety roles and
responsibilities on a residential construction site"** (WSNZ_S052) and the 2026 HSWA 2015 amendment
that introduced the statutory **critical risk** definition and the **small/large PCBU** cooperation
split. Government/regulator guidance summarised with attribution (WorkSafe ACOPs are Crown copyright,
CC BY-NC 3.0 NZ) — confirm the current edition before relying on it.

> **Not legal advice.** Following the actions in this reference that are relevant to your
> circumstances gives you **"safe harbour"** under **HSWA s 226(2)** — you're treated as having met
> the stated duty. Following the ACOP is **not mandatory**; you can meet the same duty another way, as
> long as it achieves an equal or better standard.

---

## 1. Why residential needs its own roles map

Residential sites are the segment the classic "construction project" model under-serves (→
`good-practice.md` §1): often no formal principal contractor, a white-card/site-office model doesn't
apply, and most parties are **small PCBUs** — sole traders and small building companies — working
directly for a homeowner or investor rather than inside a large managed project. The ACOP exists to
make the **HSWA roles concrete** for that setting: who is the client, who is the principal contractor,
and — distinctively for residential — **when a homeowner or landowner is a PCBU at all.**

Everyone on a residential site holds at least one of the four HSWA duty-holder roles, and a person can
hold more than one at once:

| Role | Who fills it | Health & safety focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Client** | The PCBU that commissions the residential construction work (e.g. a property developer, landlord, investor — or, in specific circumstances, a homeowner/landowner, see §3). | Engage qualified, experienced people and give them the site information they need. |
| **Principal contractor** | The PCBU with overall management/control of the site — coordinates safety across trades where more than one trade's safety is affected. Is about **control and coordination**, not doing all the work. | Set up, manage and coordinate a safe site for everyone. |
| **Contractor** | A PCBU (business or sole trader) hired to carry out part of the work — usually by the principal contractor, sometimes directly by the client. | Manage the risks from their own work and protect themselves and others. |
| **Subcontractor** | A PCBU hired by a contractor (or the principal contractor) to carry out part of the work. | Same as contractor. |
| **Officer** | A person who can significantly affect how a PCBU operates (director, partner, board member). An officer's duty **does not extend to activities they perform in another role for the same PCBU** (e.g. as a worker) → `../../officer-governance-advisor/`. | Make sure the PCBU has the systems, resources and oversight to manage health and safety well. |
| **Worker** | Anyone carrying out work on site — employees, labour-hire, subcontractors' staff, apprentices, anyone "on the tools", including a sole trader working on their own PCBU's job. | Work safely and follow reasonable instructions. |
| **Other person on site** | Present at the workplace but not working — visitors, neighbours, family, tenants not doing/commissioning the work. | Take reasonable care of their own safety, don't put workers at risk, follow reasonable instructions. |

**The contracting chain:** Client → Principal contractor → Contractor → Subcontractor. On a small job
the client and principal contractor, or the principal contractor and sole contractor, are often the
same business — that business must then meet the duties of **every** role it holds.

## 2. Finding your role — the flowchart logic

Work through in order:

1. **Are you the PCBU commissioning the work?** → **Client**.
2. **Are you the PCBU project-managing the work, the main PCBU managing the site, or the only PCBU on
   the project?** → **Principal contractor**.
3. **Are you a PCBU engaged to work on the site by another business?** → **Contractor/subcontractor**.
4. **Are you working "on the tools" on site?** → **Worker**.
5. **Are you visiting the site (not working on-site, not a PCBU)?** → **Other person on site**.
6. **Do you make decisions that significantly affect how the business operates (e.g. you own it)?** →
   **Officer** (in addition to any operational role you also hold).
7. **Are you a homeowner, or occupying a house where construction work is happening?** → go to §3.

A person/business can land in more than one row — meet the duties of each role that applies.

## 3. Homeowners, occupiers and landowners — when are they a PCBU?

This is the distinctive residential question: **a PCBU does not include a home occupier who employs
or engages a tradesperson to carry out residential work** on their own home — but several common
situations flip a homeowner into a PCBU. Work through these before defaulting to "just a homeowner":

### Building a new house to live in (residential landowner)
If you are having a house built for you, or building one yourself, you are **likely a PCBU** while the
house is being built (until you move in) — **regardless of whether you hire a business to manage it**:

- **You hire a business to project-manage the build** (they organise and coordinate the trades) → that
  business takes the **principal contractor** duties; you take the **client** actions (§4 below —
  choose capable people, share site information, don't create pressure for unsafe shortcuts). You are
  **not expected to have detailed technical knowledge of construction risks**.
- **You project-manage it yourself** (you decide which tradesperson does what, when and where, or you
  do some of the work yourself) → **you hold the principal contractor duties** (§4 below).

### Renovating or occupying an existing house
Default position: if you live in the house, your renovation work generally makes you an **"other
person at the workplace"** (HSWA s 46) while tradespeople are on site — reasonable care for your own
safety, don't put workers at risk, follow reasonable instructions. This flips to full **PCBU duties**
only if:

- **You run a business from the house** *and* **the renovation is for that business** (e.g. a
  self-employed caterer upgrading their home catering kitchen, or wiring upgrades for a home office) →
  you're a PCBU; then apply the same split as above (hire a business to manage it → you take **client**
  actions; manage it yourself → you take **principal contractor** actions).
- Renovations **unconnected to a home-based business** (a normal kitchen or bathroom upgrade, even if
  you run an unrelated business elsewhere in the house) do **not** trigger PCBU duties — you remain an
  "other person at the workplace".

### Landlords, investors and tenants
- **Renting the property out** (you don't live there) → you are a **client PCBU** in the ordinary
  sense — full client duties apply, same as any commissioning business.
- **Tenants** who are not doing or commissioning the work, and don't run a business from the residence,
  are classed as an **"other person on site"** — same reasonable-care duties as any visitor. A tenant
  who *does* run a business from the residence may pick up additional duties for that business's part
  of the work.

## 4. Actions by role — before / during / after construction

The ACOP sets out a **before construction starts / during construction / after construction** action
sequence for each role. Scale the actions to the size and complexity of the job — a four-townhouse
build needs more than a single bathroom reno — but the sequence itself applies to every residential
project.

### Client
- **Before:** choose a capable, experienced principal contractor (no formal prequalification
  required, but check experience/certifications); share site information affecting risk (asbestos,
  services, access, building plans) early enough to plan around it; with the principal contractor,
  work out roles, duties and **which risks are critical risks** where duties overlap; agree and record
  how you'll work together (who manages the site, who communicates with whom, site rules).
- **During:** don't create pressure to take unsafe shortcuts, even under time/cost/design pressure; if
  plans change, check risks are reassessed and controls confirmed before work continues; don't direct
  or control site work unless you are also the principal contractor; check in regularly that critical
  risks are controlled, issues are being addressed, and agree who notifies WorkSafe/preserves the site
  if something happens.
- **After:** take part in a project review; capture lessons learned for future projects.

### Principal contractor
- **Before:** choose capable contractors/subcontractors; agree responsibilities and, where duties
  overlap, determine the **critical risks**, each PCBU's influence/control, and how each will manage
  the matter; identify hazards/risks/control measures from the work, the site and the environment;
  plan site safety (access, traffic, facilities, high-risk deliveries, temporary works, emergency
  equipment); set procedures (visitor/worker sign-in, notifying WorkSafe, PPE, training); set up the
  site (safe access/exit/exclusion zones, inductions, work sequencing); record the site-specific health
  and safety arrangements.
- **During:** control site access and shared areas; consult/cooperate/coordinate with the other PCBUs
  on site — manage trade overlaps so work doesn't create new risks for each other; induct and involve
  workers (toolbox talks, walkthroughs); manage everyone on site (workers, visitors, deliveries,
  homeowners); monitor performance and stop work if control measures aren't working; keep managing risk
  as conditions/sequencing change; communicate changes promptly; run a clear process for notifiable
  events and site preservation.
- **After:** make the site safe to close down (remove temporary controls, manage remaining hazards,
  safe handover/occupancy access); share and use lessons learned to improve the next project's planning
  and templates.

### Contractor/subcontractor
- **Before:** choose capable workers/subcontractors, and plan for the risks of inexperienced workers
  (apprentices/trainees) and their supervision; with the principal contractor, agree roles/duties and,
  where you share a duty with another PCBU, determine **critical risks**, influence/control, and who
  manages what; identify and understand hazards/risks from your work, the site and how trades interact;
  agree site set-up and rules; prepare workers (inductions, training, PPE, safe systems of work).
- **During:** follow agreed control measures, sequencing and site rules; inform and involve your
  workers in task-specific risks; coordinate with the principal contractor and other PCBUs — attend
  toolbox talks, don't alter supplied plant/structures without authorisation, respect exclusion zones,
  stop work and tell the principal contractor if control measures aren't working, re-coordinate when
  conditions or plans change; manage the risks your own work creates and keep your area safe and tidy;
  respond to incidents/near-misses through the site process.
- **After:** leave your work area safe and tidy; hand over to the principal contractor (what's
  complete, remaining risks/special conditions, information for safe use/maintenance); take part in
  lessons-learned discussions.

### Worker
- **Before:** attend the site induction; understand the site rules (who's in charge, how to raise
  concerns/report hazards); only do work you're trained and authorised for; ask if anything is unclear.
- **During:** attend toolbox talks; understand the risks for your task; follow instructions, site rules
  and agreed controls; use PPE as trained/instructed and report if it's damaged, ill-fitting or needs
  replacing; don't alter plant or structures (e.g. scaffolding) unless trained/authorised; stay out of
  exclusion zones; **stop work and tell your contractor or the principal contractor if control measures
  aren't working**; report hazards, near misses and incidents immediately.
- **After:** leave your work area safe and tidy, secure or isolate hazards you created; return
  tools/equipment; take part in debriefs — what caused confusion, where overlaps didn't go well, what
  would make future work safer.

### Other persons on site (visitors, neighbours, tenants not doing the work)
- **During:** take reasonable care of your own health and safety and don't put workers or others at
  risk; follow site signage and instructions; stay within permitted areas; don't touch tools, plant,
  structures (e.g. scaffolding) or materials.

### Homeowners/occupiers (see §3 for which duties apply to you)
- **Before:** tell the principal contractor about property access, known issues and hazards; know who's
  in charge and how to contact them; agree when/how you can safely visit.
- **During:** take reasonable care of your own safety; follow site instructions/signage; stay within
  permitted areas; don't touch tools, plant, structures or materials.
- **After:** follow any remaining control measures while the site is cleared; ask about remaining risks
  at handover and follow guidance on using the area safely.

### Officer
- **Before:** stay well-informed about health and safety requirements and understand the business's
  operations and associated hazards/risks; provide adequate resources and processes, including in
  planning, procurement and contracting decisions (→ `../../officer-governance-advisor/` for the full
  s 44 due-diligence method).
- **During:** monitor that health and safety systems are working — incidents and non-compliance are
  managed properly and promptly, and control measures remain in place and effective.
- **After:** review how well health and safety was managed on the project; record lessons learned and
  update processes.

## 5. Overlapping duties on a residential site — cooperation scales with size

Most residential-site PCBUs are **small PCBUs (fewer than 20 workers)** — sole traders and small
trade businesses. Since the 2026 HSWA amendment, this changes what cooperation is *mandatory* (full
mechanics → `../../worksafe-nz-specialist/references/overlapping-duties.md`):

- **Large PCBU (20+ workers)** — must consult, cooperate and coordinate with other PCBUs on **all**
  relevant risks.
- **Small PCBU (< 20 workers)** — the common case on a residential job — is only **required** to
  cooperate on **critical risks**. Cooperation on other, non-critical risks is discretionary (though
  still good practice, and site rules/contracts can require it).

**In practice on a residential build:** when PCBUs first discuss how they'll work together, they
should explicitly work out (a) what risks exist from overlapping work, and (b) **which of those are
critical risks** — because that's what determines the *floor* of mandatory cooperation for the small
PCBUs who make up most residential sites. A principal contractor coordinating several small trade
PCBUs should still run broader cooperation as good practice (it's what actually prevents gaps/near
misses — see the worked examples below), but the legally mandatory minimum for each small PCBU is the
critical risks.

What counts as a **critical risk** is a statutory question, not a judgement call — see
`../../worksafe-nz-specialist/references/overlapping-duties.md` and
`../../critical-risk-manager/references/critical-risk-definitions.md` for the Schedule 1A definition.
On a residential site the Schedule 1A hazards that recur most are **work at height** (roofing,
scaffolding, ladders), **excavation** (foundations, services), **cranes/mobile plant** (deliveries,
Hiabs), and **hazardous substances** (silica dust, solvents).

### Worked patterns (illustrative, adapted from the ACOP)

- **Scaffold handover between trades** — a scaffolder sets up scaffolding for a roofing crew. Before
  climbing up, the roofing lead walks the scaffold from the ground and identifies it's not set at the
  right working height and that falling-object protection hasn't been addressed. The two PCBUs pause
  work, adjust the scaffold, add exclusion zones below it, and confirm no other trade is scheduled
  during the roof install — a critical-risk (falls, falling objects) coordination handled directly
  between two small PCBUs.
- **Sequencing around a delay** — a delivery delay stops the plumber finishing a rough-in on schedule
  just as the electrician is due to start in the same area. The principal contractor pauses the
  electrician's start, reassesses the new congestion/incomplete-services risk with both trades, and
  only restarts once controls are agreed — critical-risk reassessment triggered by a schedule change,
  not just the original plan.
- **Overhead powerlines** — a principal contractor identifies overhead lines across the site entrance
  during planning, confirms Minimum Approach Distances with the local electricity-lines authority, and
  sets a marked safe route, set-down area and exclusion zone that every trade with deliveries follows —
  a critical risk (electrical) managed proactively before any truck, Hiab or crane arrives on site
  (→ `../../electrical-energy-specialist/`).
- **An emerging hazard mid-job** — solvent fumes from bathroom waterproofing drift on an unexpected
  wind gust into an adjoining trade's work area. The affected trade stops and calls the principal
  contractor, who pauses both activities until the fumes are controlled (extraction, better
  separation) — cooperation on a critical risk (hazardous substances) responding to a changed
  condition, not the original plan.

## 6. Critical questions to consider

- Have you correctly identified **your role(s)** under §2 (and, if you're a homeowner/landowner, your
  status under §3)? A business or person can hold more than one role and must meet the duties of each.
- Is there a clear **principal contractor**, even on a small job with no formal site office?
- Where duties overlap, have the PCBUs identified **which risks are critical risks**, and confirmed who
  has the duty and how much influence/control each holds?
- If any PCBU involved is a **small PCBU (< 20 workers)** — the norm on residential sites — has
  cooperation on the **critical risks** actually happened, even if broader cooperation is informal?
- Are the **site-specific health and safety arrangements** recorded somewhere accessible (informal
  records — text messages, photos — are fine for the size of the job)?
- If a homeowner/occupier is involved, have you correctly worked out whether they're a **client PCBU**,
  a **principal contractor**, or just an **"other person at the workplace"** (§3) — and told them what
  that means for them?
- Is an **officer** of any PCBU involved also working on the tools on this job? If so, their conduct
  while doing the work is a **worker-duty** question, not an officer-duty question →
  `../../officer-governance-advisor/`.
