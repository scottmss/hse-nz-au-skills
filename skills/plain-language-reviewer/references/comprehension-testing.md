# Testing whether the document actually works

Reading a document and understanding it are different things, and a signature on a register proves
neither. This file covers the draft cycle, the two comprehension-check methods, alternate formats,
and how to tell whether the document is working once it is out.

> Summarised from the WorkSafe NZ GPG *Writing for health and safety* (Nov 2017).
> **Sources last verified: 2026-09-04.** **Not legal advice.**

## The draft cycle

### First draft — ask for feedback
Invite workers to read it and tell you how it can be improved. Useful questions:

- **Does it have the information you need?** Is it correct? Up to date? Are the work processes
  described accurately? Is anything missing? Is there anything you do **not** need?
- **Can you understand it?** If not, **which parts** are hard, and **why** are they hard?
- **Is it useful?** Why, or why not?
- **What are the main points?** What did you learn from reading it? *(If the answer does not match
  your intended main messages, the document has failed — regardless of how well it reads.)*
- **Is the information in a logical order?** If not, how would you order it?
- **Does it make you want to read it?**

### Second draft — close the loop
Tell workers **how and where you used their feedback**. Explain why some information was included
and other information was not, and why anything new was added. Skipping this is how organisations
teach workers that consultation is theatre.

### Final draft
Settle layout, design and graphics. Then have someone **who has not seen it** proofread it. Run the
checklist in `review-checklist.md`, and follow your workplace's quality assurance, document
checking, version control and approval processes.

## The two comprehension checks

Both work on the same principle: **if what the worker does or says does not match the document, the
document is wrong.** Not the worker. That framing matters — the moment a check becomes a test of
the person, you stop learning anything about the document.

Open it honestly. Something like:

> *"Here is a new document. I'd like you to read it and help me check it is clear. If you don't
> understand a part, I need you to show me where, so I can fix it."*

### Demonstration
Ask a worker to **show you the steps as they read through the document**. Watch what they actually
do. Where their actions diverge from the text, stop and talk about their understanding of that
step, so you learn *where* and *how* to rewrite it.

Best for procedures, work instructions, and anything with a physical sequence.

### Thinking aloud
Ask the worker to read **a short piece at a time**, and after each piece, say what they think it
means. Prompt with an example first so they know what you are asking for:

> *"Please read this part and tell me what you think it is saying. For example, this part is about
> pre-start checks for your forklift — when you get to it, I need you to say to me, 'this says I
> have to do these things…'"*

Where their reading does not match your intent, explain what you were trying to say and **ask them
what words they would use instead**. Their words are usually the right ones — that is the whole
point of writing for the reader.

Best for policies, information sheets and anything without a physical sequence.

## Alternate formats

Reading difficulty is only one reason a document does not reach someone. Workers may be blind or
have low vision, have an intellectual or learning disability, be unable to hold a document or turn
pages, or have no practical access to the intranet where it lives.

**Ask workers what format works for them.** Options:

- **Audio** — MP3 or recorded walk-through. Note that some workers cannot use audio either; never
  assume a single alternate format covers everyone.
- **Captioned video** — makes spoken content accessible to deaf and hearing-impaired workers, and
  is useful for everyone in a noisy workplace.
- **Braille** — anything printable can be produced in braille, including labels, forms and signs.
- **Large print and high contrast** — the cheapest and most commonly needed adjustment.
- **Images, photo sequences and diagrams** — often the strongest option for a multilingual
  workforce, because they work for every language at once.
- **A one-page summary** attached to the front of a long document.

## Is the document effective once it is out?

Decide in advance **how you will know**, and record it. Reading it out at a toolbox meeting is not
evidence that it works.

- **Watch workers use it.** Do they do what it says? Then talk about whether it worked for them.
- **Has it been shared with all workers** who need it?
- **Have they been trained** in the new policy, process or procedure?
- **Is it being used the way you expected?** How is it being used? If it is not being used, **why
  not?** The answer is usually a design fault, not a discipline problem.
- **How did workers react to it?**
- **What effect has it had on behaviour?**

Review it with workers as part of the annual H&S review cycle.

## Updating an existing document

Review it with the questions above **first**, then look for what has changed:

- changes to the **H&S system** — new hazards, controls or practices
- changes to the **law**
- changes to **equipment, plant or products**
- changes to the **people** doing the work, or their mix of languages and reading levels

Record the **date of the change, the version number, or both**, on the document itself:

> *Chainsaw Safety Policy — Version 3, updated 5 February 2026*

An H&S document with no version stamp rots silently, and no one can tell whether the copy in the
crib room is the current one. Where the document is an SOP, the full document-control conventions
are in `../../sop-author/references/sop-structure.md`.

## Sharing and introducing it

Decide **who introduces it and talks it through** — supervisors, managers, H&S representatives, H&S
committee members, or union representatives. A document that arrives by email attachment with no
introduction has been distributed, not communicated.
