# Plain language — New Zealand anchor (WorkSafe GPG) and the AU position

The region-specific layer for `plain-language-reviewer`. The method is jurisdiction-neutral and
lives in `plain-language-method.md`; this file records the source document, the duties it connects
to, and what legal weight it does and does not carry.

## The source document

**Writing for health and safety — guidance for workplace health and safety writers**, WorkSafe New
Zealand **good practice guidelines**, **November 2017**.

- Landing page:
  https://www.worksafe.govt.nz/tools-registers-resources/resources/writing-health-and-safety-documents-for-your-workplace/
- PDF:
  https://www.worksafe.govt.nz/dmsdocument/2188-writing-for-health-and-safety-guidance-for-workplace-health-and-safety-writers/

Structure: five questions (what is the document for; who will read it; what are the main messages;
is it effective; how will you share and use it), then seven appendices — instruction-document
guidelines, alternate formats, a simple-words list, plain-English explanations of key H&S terms,
worked plain-English examples, comprehension-check methods, and a full document checklist.

**Note the date.** This is a **2017** document, still published by WorkSafe as current guidance.
The writing method has not dated, but check anything it says about the regulatory landscape against
current law before repeating it, and expect its statutory cross-references to have moved.

**Co-authorship is worth knowing.** WorkSafe acknowledges **Workbase** (the adult literacy body)
alongside ACC, NZ Transport Agency, NZISM, the Crane Association of NZ, the NZ Contractors'
Federation, ECANZ, Downer, Powerco, Impac and Write Limited. This is not a style guide written by
communications staff; it is grounded in workplace adult-literacy practice and industry input, which
is why its comprehension-testing methods are the strongest part of it.

## Legal weight

**Plain language is not a legal standard.** Nothing in NZ or AU law prescribes a reading level, a
sentence length or a readability score, and **no score discharges any duty**. Never present a
readability figure as compliance, and never let a rewrite substitute for the accuracy check in
`review-checklist.md`.

What plain language *does* is discharge duties that already exist:

### NZ

| Instrument | What it requires | Why it matters here |
|---|---|---|
| **HSWA 2015** — primary duty | Ensure health and safety **so far as is reasonably practicable** | A control the worker cannot understand is not an effective control |
| **HSW (General Risk and Workplace Management) Regulations 2016, reg 9** | The duty to provide **information, supervision, training and instruction** — workers only do tasks they have adequate knowledge or experience for, or are adequately supervised | The GPG cites this regulation as the hook for its guidance, and states that training must be **easy for workers to understand**. Treat the "easy to understand" phrasing as the **GPG's gloss** — verify the regulation's own words before quoting them as statutory text |
| **HSWA 2015** — worker engagement, participation and representation | Engage with workers on matters affecting their health and safety, and have practices for ongoing participation | Drafting *with* workers rather than issuing *at* them is this duty doing real work |
| **WorkSafe GPGs generally** | The regulator's view of good practice | Persuasive on what "reasonably practicable" looks like; not binding law |

The practical formulation: **a document workers cannot understand is not information they have been
given.** That is the argument this skill exists to make, and it is a stronger one than "it reads
badly".

### Australia

There is **no Safe Work Australia equivalent** to this GPG — but the AU duty is arguably stated
more directly than the NZ one.

- **Model WHS Regulations, reg 39** — information, training and instruction provided to a worker
  must be **suitable and adequate** having regard to the nature of the work, the nature of the
  risks, and the control measures implemented; and, **so far as is reasonably practicable**, must
  be provided in a way that is **readily understandable by any person to whom it is provided**
  (reg 39(3)).
- That "readily understandable" test attaches to **how information is delivered**, not merely to
  whether delivery happened — which is precisely the gap this skill closes.
- The model **Codes of Practice** repeat the expectation in their own contexts, and Victoria runs
  its **separate OHS Act 2004** and compliance codes. Confirm the regulation number in the
  applicable jurisdiction — states adopt the model regulations with their own numbering and
  variations. See `../../safework-au-specialist/`.

**For AU work, cite reg 39, not the CHASNZ-style NZ guidance.** Use the method here, attribute the
duty locally.

## Multilingual workforces — a duty question, not a courtesy

Both jurisdictions land in the same place: if a worker cannot understand the information, the duty
is not met. That makes the translation cautions in `plain-language-method.md` operative rather than
advisory —

- do not assume a worker who **speaks** a language can **read** it;
- use a **qualified translator**, not a bilingual colleague;
- check the **regional dialect**, including for **te reo Māori**;
- and prefer **images and demonstration** where translation is uncertain, because they carry the
  message without depending on literacy in any language.
